# Shihua Scrolls · English Guide

**Classical Chinese poetry as living ink-painting scrolls.** Poets rarely say "I miss you." They paint a willow, a lone fortress or a misty canal, and let the scene carry the feeling.

[▶ Open the scrolls](https://tuanziding.github.io/shihua/) · [简体中文](../README.md)

## The core idea

When we part, we shake hands and then wave. A willow by the water, swaying in the wind, looks like a host's arm: reluctant to let go, slowly waving goodbye. In Chinese, *liu* (willow, 柳) sounds like *liu* (stay, 留), so breaking a willow branch at a farewell became a quiet way to say "please stay." This restraint (含蓄) and direct expression are two equally moving traditions.

## Three scrolls

| Scroll | Folder | Opening poem | What's inside |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Willow · Farewell Unspoken | `yangliu-yiyi/` | *Caiwei*, Book of Songs | How a poem begins → willow means "stay" → handshake, letting go, waving (animated riverside farewell) → two languages of goodbye |
| Pass · Frontier Poems | `biansai/` | Wang Changling, *Out of the Frontier* | Five images, then 18 poems in four scrolls: desert landscapes, valor, homesickness, compassion for war |
| Dream · Jiangnan Water-Town Poems | `jiangnan/` | Bai Juyi, *Remembering Jiangnan* | Six images, then 16 poems in four scrolls: spring river, canals and bridges, West Lake lotus, misty sorrow |

## Notes

English text is an interpretive guide, not a literal translation. Poems follow standard editions; a few characters have textual variants. Paintings are AI-generated. Original poems are public domain; paintings, code and guide text are shared under CC BY-NC 4.0 for non-commercial learning.
